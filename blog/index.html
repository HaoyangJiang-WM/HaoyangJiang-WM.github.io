A Graph Is Not a Closed System

What remains when the environment is left outside the graph?

Haoyang Jiang · William & Mary · Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 16, 2026

To represent a system as a graph, we choose a set of entities and a set of relations. That choice tells us what is inside our representation. It does not tell us whether the represented system is isolated from everything left outside.

This distinction becomes consequential when a graph is used to predict dynamics. The observed vertices may be influenced by processes that are not represented as vertices, by interactions that cross the chosen boundary, and by an environment that responds to the system itself. None of these effects disappears when its external nodes disappear from our data.

Choosing the nodes of a graph chooses what we represent. It does not, by itself, choose a physically valid model of everything we leave out.

The underlying question is therefore not whether a graph neural network can attach another feature to a node. Of course it can. It is what relationship we assume between the graph we represent and the world we do not.

This post develops that idea from the mathematics of open systems. It distinguishes a graph's representation boundary from the boundary of its physical influence, asks what survives when we eliminate the exterior, and identifies the different questions this creates for learning.

1. What a graph leaves out

A graph \(G=(V,E)\) specifies which entities and relations we retain. A graph predictor may receive these relations, the observed state \(\mathbf{x}\), and perhaps a history of such states. For fixed parameters, a deterministic predictor necessarily gives the same output for the same inputs.

\[\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{t+h}=F_\theta\!\left(G,\mathbf{x}_{t-k:t}\right)\]

But drawing a finite graph is not the same as declaring that it is dynamically closed. If the retained vertices are a subset \(V\) of a larger physical network, interactions crossing from the omitted exterior \(W\) into \(V\) still exist. The induced subgraph does not, by itself, tell us what happens at that cut.

Where does information come from? The modeled state may be affected by internal interactions, observed boundary inputs, and unobserved exterior processes. These are different sources of physical influence. Their distinction is easy to lose when all data are packed into a single node-feature tensor.

Nodes are not the problem: a neural network can attach a boundary measurement to an existing node, use global features, or employ an auxiliary state. The problem is whether the relevant boundary mechanism—and the information available at prediction time—has been specified.

2. Three ways to cut the same physical network

Consider linear diffusion on a finite undirected conductance network. Partition the full state into retained states \(\mathbf{x}\) and exterior states \(\mathbf{z}\). With unit interior storage capacity and a positive exterior capacity \(\tau\), write

\[\begin{aligned}\dot{\mathbf{x}}&=-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{z},\\[2pt]\tau\dot{\mathbf{z}}&=\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{D}\mathbf{z},\end{aligned}\]

Notation throughout: bold lowercase letters denote state/input vectors, bold uppercase letters denote matrices, and nonbold italic letters denote scalar quantities. Subscripts \(I\) and \(E\) mark retained and exterior blocks; \(\mathcal{B}\) denotes a boundary-correction matrix. Here \(\mathbf{C}\) contains nonnegative cross-boundary conductances, \(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{in}}\) is the Laplacian of the retained edges, and \(\mathbf{1}\) is an all-ones vector of matching dimension. Define

\[\begin{aligned}\mathbf{A}&=\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{in}}+\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1}),\\[2pt]\mathbf{D}&=\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{out}}+\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{1}),\end{aligned}\]

Assume each exterior component touches the retained network so that \(\mathbf{D}\) is invertible. We can now make three very different modeling choices.

Delete the crossing edges

\[\dot{\mathbf{x}}=-\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{in}}\mathbf{x}\]

The cut has no flow across it. In this diffusion model, we have imposed an insulating boundary.

Clamp the exterior

\[\dot{\mathbf{x}}=-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{g}(t)\]

We keep the cross-boundary conductances but prescribe an exterior state z = g(t). Even g = 0 does not mean no flow.

Eliminate a static exterior

\[\dot{\mathbf{x}}=-\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{K}}\mathbf{x}\]

The exterior is assumed to equilibrate instantaneously, giving the Schur-complement operator below.

\[\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{K}}=\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf T}\]

This is the structure of Kron reduction. It can introduce effective couplings between retained vertices that were not adjacent in the induced subgraph. It preserves a specified static terminal behavior; it does not generally preserve the transients of an exterior with finite storage.

Restricting a graph is not the same operation as reducing a physical system.

Indeed, whenever positive-conductance crossing edges exist, the Laplacian of the induced subgraph differs from the principal block of the full Laplacian: the latter retains the diagonal contributions of the missing edges. This is a statement about this physical diffusion operator, not about every arbitrary GNN layer.

3. When the exterior has dynamics, it can leave memory

For finite \(\tau\), the exterior has its own state and does not equilibrate instantaneously. Solving its differential equation and substituting into the retained system gives an exact reduction:

\[\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t)+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\tau}(t)+\int_0^t\mathbf{K}_{\tau}(t-s)\mathbf{x}(s)\,\mathrm ds\]
\[\begin{aligned}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\tau}(t)&=\mathbf{C}e^{-\mathbf{D}t/\tau}\mathbf{z}_0,\\[2pt]\mathbf{K}_{\tau}(t)&=\frac1\tau\mathbf{C}e^{-\mathbf{D}t/\tau}\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf T},\end{aligned}\]

The exterior contributes both its initial condition and a response to the retained system’s history. Different boundary sites can become coupled through pathways that lie entirely outside the retained graph. This is direct elimination of an omitted graph subsystem, not a question of spatial discretization.

In the Laplace domain (for zero exterior initial state), the same effect becomes an operator acting on boundary variables:

\[\mathbf{Y}_{\partial}(s)=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1})-\mathbf{C}(\tau s\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{D})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\mathsf T}\]

A fixed weighted edge may be enough to represent a static boundary relation. A frequency-dependent terminal response generally requires a time-domain memory operator, auxiliary states, or an approximation. The Mori–Zwanzig / optimal-prediction literature provides a broader context for why eliminating dynamical degrees of freedom can lead to memory terms.

Critically, an environment is not always a prerecorded input. When it responds to the subsystem, reusing a measured boundary signal from another trajectory after changing the interior need not preserve the original coupled physics.

4. What survives when the exterior is left out?

Leaving an exterior region outside the graph does not specify how that region should be treated. Three logically distinct effects may remain, even when none of the exterior's internal nodes is explicitly represented.

Effective relations

The omitted environment changes how retained states influence one another. A reduced system may acquire new couplings between nodes that have no direct edge in the observed subgraph.

External driving

The environment can inject a signal or intervention that is not determined by the current retained state. Knowing the graph's internal connectivity does not tell us the value of that signal.

Dynamic feedback

The exterior may react to the retained system and later feed back the effects of its history. Eliminating the exterior can therefore produce memory rather than a fixed boundary value.

These are not competing names for the same phenomenon. An effective operator describes a relationship, an external forcing describes a realized influence, and memory describes dependence on past interaction. A physical model can involve more than one of them.

A useful principle follows: the absence of external nodes does not imply the absence of external structure. Such structure may be represented by interface laws, reduced operators, latent dynamics, or uncertainty, depending on what is known and what the task requires.

This is why boundary-aware graph learning is already a meaningful part of the literature. For example, Suzuki and Hisakado (2026) show, in a specific physical setting, that external boundary conditions can alter the graph operator and the basis used by a spectral GNN. Their result supports the broader conceptual distinction; our question here is not tied to their particular physical variables or architecture.

5. Open graphs: making the outside an explicit interface

Mathematical physics has long treated networks as open systems. Its key move is conceptual: define the internal network, identify the places where it interacts with its surroundings, and state which variables or relations govern those interactions.

In port-Hamiltonian systems on graphs, this separation is made precise through boundary ports and conservation-compatible interconnections. An interface is not an assertion that every external entity must become an extra internal node. Rather, it describes how the represented subsystem can exchange influence with something outside it.

That viewpoint gives us three important distinctions:

What we knowWhat it does not automatically tell us
Internal topology: which retained entities are relatedThe external interactions acting on those entities
Boundary law: how an interface respondsThe actual external input realized in a particular forecast
Boundary observations: what has been measuredWhether the unknown exterior dynamics can be uniquely recovered

Existing work already develops individual pieces, including mixed-boundary graph PDE solvers (Horie and Mitsume, 2022) and compositional port-based learning (Neary and Topcu, 2023). The purpose of bringing them together here is not to claim a new algorithm, but to ask whether external interaction should be made explicit when we define a graph learning problem.

A graph describes internal relationships. An open-system representation also asks how the outside can act on the inside.

6. A minimal thought experiment: same graph, different boundaries

To make the distinction testable, consider a minimal linear network: two retained states are coupled to each other and to one omitted state. Its exterior response time is controlled by \(\tau\). Changing \(\tau\) changes the physical system, while all four models below retain the same observed two-node graph. The numerical setup uses internal conductance \(1/4\), two exterior links of conductance \(1\), and initial state \(\mathbf{x}(0)=(2,0)^{\mathsf T}\), \(z(0)=0\).

For the full system the first retained state has a closed form:

\[x_1(t)=\frac{2}{2+\tau}+\frac{\tau}{2+\tau}e^{-(1+2/\tau)t}+e^{-3t/2}\]

As \(\tau\) tends to zero, the static reduction becomes accurate in this example. The magnitude of its exact state error in either retained coordinate is

\[\delta_{\tau}(t)=\frac{\tau}{2+\tau}\left(1-e^{-(1+2/\tau)t}\right)\]

The practical lesson is not “always add memory.” It is: choose the simplest exterior model that preserves the behavior needed for the task.

Separate question: what if the boundary forcing is unobserved?

The earlier three-gauge demonstration is still useful, but it tests an information limit, not whether a particular boundary closure is correct. Two different forcing schedules can produce different futures from identical graph observations, while an input-blind point predictor must give the same forecast for both scenarios. For three observed gauges and a constant upstream boundary input \(b\), the discrete-time experiment uses

\[x_i(n+1)=\tfrac12x_i(n)+\tfrac12x_{i-1}(n),\qquad x_0(n)=b,\quad \mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{0},\quad i=1,2,3.\]

The midpoint curve is optimal only under the stated equal-probability, squared-error assumptions. A predictive distribution could correctly represent both futures. This does not prove a deficiency unique to GNNs.

7. What can a graph learner actually know?

We should distinguish at least three situations: a known imposed boundary input, a partially observed input, and an interacting environment with unknown internal state. They are not the same learning problem.

Modeling questionWhat needs to be specified?
RepresentationWhich nodes, edges and external interfaces have been retained?
ClosureWhat relation replaces the omitted environment: prescribed input, static response, dynamic feedback or uncertainty?
ObservationWhat boundary measurements and future controls are available when the prediction is made?
IdentificationWhich response parameters or hidden states can actually be identified from those observations?

Even perfect observations of the interface need not identify the exterior's entire topology. Different networks can have the same terminal behavior. The appropriate target may be a predictive boundary response, not reconstruction of every hidden node.

Likewise, if both internal dynamics and external driving are unconstrained, they may not be separately identifiable from one observed trajectory. Physical knowledge, external measurements and interventions can make the distinction testable.

When future forcing is not yet observable, a learned history encoder may estimate correlated latent factors but cannot know an independent intervention chosen after the forecast origin. A calibrated distribution may be more appropriate than a single point estimate.

8. What would count as progress in graph learning?

The experiments above validate mathematical identities; they do not rank GNN architectures. A convincing learning comparison should hold available information constant while changing representation or inductive bias.

QuestionFair comparison
Does more boundary evidence help?Internal-only inputs versus the same inputs plus measured boundary information. Attribute gains to new information.
Does an explicit interface help?Boundary-conditioned MPNN / recurrent baseline versus port-structured model, with identical observations and budgets.
Does learned dynamics survive repartitioning?Model the same physical network under different subgraph cuts, preserving the appropriate cross-boundary coupling law.
When is a simple closure enough?Compare imposed-input and static/Kron baselines against dynamic models over time-scale regimes where each is justified.

A model can conserve the total quantity yet move it through the wrong interface. It can also satisfy a passivity constraint while predicting an incorrect trajectory. We should report prediction error, interface-law consistency, conservation or energy balance where applicable, and uncertainty calibration when external forcing is unknown.

Existing work already addresses important pieces: Horie and Mitsume (2022) study graph PDE solvers with mixed boundary conditions; Neary and Topcu (2023) study compositional port-Hamiltonian learning; and Suzuki and Hisakado (2026) construct a boundary-aware graph Laplacian and spectral GNN for circuits. These are precedents, not peripheral exceptions.

The boundary is a modeling decision

A graph can describe a subsystem perfectly well. The mistake begins when the chosen vertex set is also treated as a complete account of that subsystem's environment.

Removing exterior vertices may leave a prescribed input, a static terminal relation, a dynamic response with memory, or uncertainty about unmeasured forces. The right description depends on the physics, observations and prediction task—not on the name of a neural architecture.

When we draw the boundary of a graph, what model of the outside world have we silently chosen?
