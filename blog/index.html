A Graph Is Not a Closed System

What does a graph leave out—and what happens when the outside world still matters?

Haoyang Jiang · William & Mary · Updated November 16, 2026

A graph is a remarkably economical representation: a set of entities, together with the relations between them. But the moment we use that representation to predict a physical or dynamical process, we must ask a question that topology alone cannot answer: does the graph contain everything that can influence its future?

Not necessarily. The vertices we choose to observe can be a small part of a larger interacting system. Processes outside that choice can act on the vertices inside it, even though the omitted region has no representation of its own.

A boundary of representation is not necessarily a boundary of physical influence.

This is not an argument that GNNs cannot receive external features: they can. It is an argument that the choice of a graph and the choice of a dynamically adequate model are different choices. Mathematical physics has long taken that distinction seriously; graph learning should, too.

1. What does a graph say?

Let \(G=(V,E)\) describe the represented vertices and their internal relations. A model may attach observations \(\mathbf{x}\) to those vertices. The graph states which interactions have been included; it does not certify that every causally relevant interaction has been included.

Imagine that the represented vertices lie inside a larger network \(\widetilde G\), and that other vertices lie outside the selected set \(V\). Connections across that cut are genuine interactions, although they no longer appear among the edges of the induced subgraph \(\widetilde G[V]\).

Crucially, an endpoint or low-degree vertex in an observed graph is not automatically a physical boundary. Whether a boundary exists, where it is located, and what crosses it are facts about the larger system and our modeling choice—not properties that can generally be inferred from degrees alone.

The first question, Where does the information come from?, is thus deeper than asking how many message-passing layers we use. We should first ask whether relevant external conditions entered the representation at all.

2. When is the represented state enough?

We can express the central issue without committing to a particular neural network or physical domain.

Let \(s\) denote the state of the complete system, let \(\Phi_t(s)\) be its deterministic evolution, and let \(P(s)\) retain only the state represented on our graph. Assume a fixed represented graph \(G\); its identity may be included in \(P\) when comparing different graphs.

We seek an autonomous, exact evolution law \(F_t\) on the retained state alone, satisfying

\[P\circ\Phi_t=F_t\circ P.\]

Such a law is well-defined if and only if, on the states and times under consideration, two complete states that look the same inside the graph also evolve to the same represented state:

\[\boxed{\ P(s_1)=P(s_2)\ \Longrightarrow\ P(\Phi_t(s_1))=P(\Phi_t(s_2))\ }.\]

The reason is simple. If the retained states coincide but the retained futures differ, \(F_t\) would have to send one input to two distinct outputs. Conversely, if the implication holds, define \(F_t(P(s))=P(\Phi_t(s))\): the result does not depend on which complete state was chosen.

This is an elementary closure / factorization criterion from dynamical systems, not a new theorem about GNNs. It says that an exact graph-state-only forecast requires the retained variables to be sufficient for the requested evolution. Adding history, a measured boundary signal, or latent variables changes what the model knows, and may make a useful closure possible.

3. A three-node thought experiment

Consider two connected states, \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), that we include in the graph. Both also interact with a third state \(z\) that we decide not to represent. The interactions could describe transport, diffusion or any comparable exchange mechanism; no particular application is needed.

At one moment, the two visible states are identical in two possible worlds:

\[(x_1,x_2)=(0,0),\qquad z=0\ \text{or}\ z=1.\]

Suppose the external state contributes \(b(z-x_i)\) to the rate of change of each visible state, where \(b>0\). When \(z=0\), the contribution is zero; when \(z=1\), it is \(b\). The visible graph and its visible state are the same, yet the instantaneous futures are different.

The graph is not wrong. It correctly records what we chose to include. The mistake would be to assume that this representation alone necessarily determines its future.

Notice also what this example does not say: if \(z\) were known, attaching it as a feature to the existing nodes could be a perfectly adequate solution. No special GNN architecture is ruled out or required by this example.

4. What can remain after the exterior is removed?

Removing the exterior from our description is not the same thing as removing its influence. The essential insight from network reduction is that the omitted region can leave several different kinds of traces.

External driving

The exterior can supply a signal that is not determined by current graph states. We may know it, measure it imperfectly, or have to represent uncertainty about it.

Effective relations

Paths through the omitted region can create effective interactions between retained vertices. A subgraph can therefore have different effective dynamics from the original graph restricted to those vertices.

Dynamic memory

An interacting environment can respond to the represented system and later feed that response back. Eliminating its states may produce dependence on the past, not merely one new constant.

For intuition, take a linear dynamical network with retained state \(\mathbf{x}\) and exterior state \(\mathbf{z}\). Use bold lowercase letters for vectors, bold uppercase letters for matrices, and italic letters for scalars:

\[\begin{aligned} \dot{\mathbf{x}}&=-\mathbf A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf C\mathbf z,\\ \tau\dot{\mathbf z}&=\mathbf C^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x-\mathbf D\mathbf z, \end{aligned}\]

Here \(\tau>0\) controls the exterior response time and \(\mathbf D\) is assumed to have eigenvalues with positive real parts. If the exterior is instantaneously equilibrated, it can yield an effective operator \(\mathbf A-\mathbf C\mathbf D^{-1}\mathbf C^{\mathsf T}\). If the exterior really evolves in time, solving for \(\mathbf z\) and substituting it back leaves an initial-condition term and a memory integral:

\[\dot{\mathbf x}(t)=-\mathbf A\mathbf x(t)+\mathbf C e^{-\mathbf D t/\tau}\mathbf z_0+ \int_0^t\frac{\mathbf C e^{-\mathbf D(t-s)/\tau}\mathbf C^{\mathsf T}}{\tau}\mathbf x(s)\,\mathrm ds.\]

These are classical linear-system identities, not proposed new architectures. They demonstrate a conceptual point: the exterior may be absent as a set of nodes, while remaining present as an effective law, a hidden initial condition, or a history-dependent response.

They also explain why different reductions are not equivalent. Deleting crossing edges, fixing exterior states, assuming instantaneous equilibrium, and retaining dynamic feedback express different models of the outside world. A simple reduction may be accurate when its assumptions fit; no extra complexity is universally necessary.

5. What open-system theory teaches us

The mathematical idea of an open graph predates graph neural networks. Rather than pretending a finite network contains all of reality, the model distinguishes internal structure from interfaces through which it interacts with other systems.

In Port-Hamiltonian Systems on Graphs, for instance, boundary variables describe exchanges between an internal network and its environment while respecting suitable conservation and power-balance laws. Those interfaces may be attached to boundary vertices, but their physical role is different from an ordinary internal neighbor relation.

Other traditions lead to the same modeling lesson through different mathematics: Kron reduction studies effective boundary relations in networks, while the Mori–Zwanzig viewpoint clarifies how eliminating dynamic variables may leave memory. These theories have distinct assumptions and should not be collapsed into one universal equation.

Graph learning has already incorporated some of these ideas. Horie and Mitsume (2022) study graph PDE solvers with mixed boundary conditions; Neary and Topcu (2023) study compositional port-Hamiltonian learning; and Suzuki and Hisakado (2026) develop a boundary-aware graph representation and spectral GNN in a specific physical setting.

So the point is not that boundary-aware graph learning is new, or that nodes fundamentally forbid it. The point is that external interaction should be an explicit modeling question rather than an assumption silently answered by the selected node set.

7. What changes for graph learning?

This perspective does not force a new layer, a new node type, or even a new name for a research field. It asks us to describe the learning problem more carefully along three axes:

QuestionWhat must be made explicit?
RepresentationWhich internal relations and external interfaces does the model represent?
ClosureIs the omitted environment treated as a known input, an effective relation, a hidden dynamic state, or uncertainty?
InformationWhat can actually be observed or known when the forecast is made—including future controls and boundary measurements?

Two cautions follow. First, a boundary interface is a representation mechanism, not a guarantee of new observations. A latent or auxiliary node that is computed only from the original inputs cannot magically reveal an independent future intervention. Second, observing a boundary response need not identify the unique topology of the hidden environment; different exteriors can induce the same behavior at the interface.

A fair model comparison should therefore distinguish the benefit of providing more information from the benefit of representing the same information differently. Boundary-conditioned node-feature baselines are important; so are comparisons across different ways of cutting the same physical system.

Graph learning should ask not only how information moves among represented nodes, but also what permits information to enter from outside their representation.

The boundary is a modeling decision

There is nothing inherently wrong with representing only part of a larger system. A graph is useful precisely because we can select a finite structure to reason about. But that selection and the dynamical closure needed for prediction are separate decisions.

Sometimes a boundary input is sufficient. Sometimes a static effective relation is appropriate. Sometimes an omitted environment feeds back through memory, or its future influence remains uncertain. The right answer depends on the system, the observable information, and the prediction question.

When the graph ends, does the system end with it? Usually, that is a question we have not yet answered merely by drawing the graph.
